7.1 · Where on Earth Is All the Water?

The Hydrosphere

The hydrosphere is all the places on Earth that hold water. Water collects in reservoirs — parts of Earth where it remains for a period of time — and the ocean is by far the largest.

  • Reservoirs: the ocean holds the most water — about 75% of global precipitation falls straight back into it.
  • Aquifers: water accumulates in subsurface rock and sediment, then seeps toward oceans, lakes, and rivers at roughly 1 ft/year.
  • Residence time: how long a water molecule stays in a reservoir — about 10 days in the atmosphere, but thousands of years in the deep ocean.
  • Embedded water: the water used to make goods — ~700 gallons for a gallon of beer, 500+ gallons for a pound of pork.
  • Surface water: rivers, streams, lakes, and wetlands are the most available fresh water for human use; glaciers are masses of ice that flow over land.
  • Groundwater recharge: water soaking down through the soil into aquifers, replenishing groundwater.
  • Transpiration: plants release water vapor into the atmosphere through their leaves.

7.3 · How Do Humans Impact Fresh Water? — Flint, Michigan

When Cost-Cutting Poisoned a City

To cut costs while waiting for a new pipeline, Flint switched its water source in 2014 — a decision with severe consequences.

  • The new, more corrosive water source released lead from aging pipes and plumbing fixtures.
  • Dangerous levels of lead turned up in water sampled from homes and schools across Flint.
  • Lead exposure causes irreversible neurological damage — especially harmful to children's developing brains.

7.3 · Groundwater Depletion and Surface-Water Disruption

Mining Water We Cannot Replace

Water mining means withdrawing groundwater faster than it is replenished. This excessive groundwater withdrawal can lead to aquifer depletion.

10×
growth in global groundwater use since 1950
  • Renewable vs. non-renewable: some aquifers recharge in years; others hold “fossil” water that is, for practical purposes, gone once used.
  • Subsidence: the ground sinks or collapses as water drains from aquifers — often permanently.
  • Impervious surfaces (roads, rooftops): send water to storm drains instead of soil, worsening floods, stream-bank erosion, and habitat damage.
  • Dams and diversions: disrupt fish migration and sediment transport.

7.3 · Dams and Diversions

Managing Water Flows at Civilizational Scale

A constant supply of water and power — paid for downstream.

Dams store water through dry seasons and generate hydroelectric power, and diversions carry that water to cities and farms. The costs fall on the river below the dam and on the people and ecosystems that depend on it.

7.3 · Water Pollution

Where Pollution Comes From

Regulation is easiest when you can point to the pipe.

Easier to regulate
Point sources

Contaminants from a clearly identifiable conduit — a pipe, ditch, channel, or well. The source is known, so it can be permitted and monitored.

vs.
Harder to regulate
Nonpoint sources

Pollutants from broad, diffuse places — agricultural, residential, and industrial activities across a whole watershed.

What the water carries
i
Sediment pollution

Loose soil swept into waterways makes the water turbid (cloudy) and reduces light penetration.

ii
Chemical & biological

Chemicals from agriculture, energy, and infrastructure (as in Flint); bacterial and viral contamination, including feedlot runoff.

7.4 · Can We Improve Access to Fresh Water?

Water Scarcity and Conservation

More than 2 billion people suffer from water scarcity. The UN's goal of water security means reliable access to safe, affordable water for everyone.

2 billion+
people who suffer from water scarcity
  • In parts of Africa, over half the population lacks safe drinking water — some walk three hours a day to collect it.
  • California's 2015 drought led to mandatory 25% water cuts.
  • In Cape Town, wealthier residents kept home water tanks while poorer residents waited in long lines at public distribution centers.
  • Some Middle Eastern nations without adequate fresh water have invested heavily in desalination.

7.4 · Water Conservation — What Works?

Conservation as the First Line of Defense

  • Not watering lawns and reducing landscape irrigation have large cumulative impacts at the city scale.
  • Regulating agricultural irrigation — with “smart” systems that release small, targeted amounts only when needed — can be even more effective.
  • Farmers can switch to less thirsty crops, and water recycling treats residential water for reuse in agriculture or industry.
  • Harvesting rainwater and desalination are additional solutions to water scarcity.

7.5 · How Do We Keep Fresh Water Clean?

Treatment, Access, and Regulation

Even where treatment works well, gaps remain. In the US, more than 90% of people are served by a water system with no violations — yet some communities still face unsafe water.

  • Globally, 2.5 billion people lack sanitary wastewater systems, and 1.8 billion drink water contaminated with sewage.
  • US framework: the Clean Water Act sets surface-water quality standards; the Safe Drinking Water Act sets public-supply standards.
  • Agricultural nonpoint-source pollution remains the most challenging to regulate.
  • Vegetated buffer zones: strips of trees, shrubs, and grasses along streams trap sediment and absorb fertilizer runoff before it reaches the water, one of the most effective ways to protect water quality.
  • New ideas: green roofs and the solar “watercone” that distills clean water using sunlight.

7.8 · How Can Humans Impact the Ocean?

Garbage, Fossil-Fuel Pollution, and Runoff

Human activity harms the ocean in three big ways — plastic garbage that breaks into toxic microplastics, fossil-fuel pollution from spills and airborne mercury, and agricultural runoff that creates oxygen-starved “dead zones.”

7.8 · How Can Humans Impact the Ocean?

Overfishing, Climate Change, and Acidification

Three more pressures push ocean ecosystems toward collapse: overfishing faster than stocks can recover, a warming climate that disrupts food webs, and acidification as the ocean absorbs CO₂ and forms carbonic acid that dissolves shells.

★ 7.9 · What Can I Do?

Protecting Fresh and Salt Water

ASU’s Global Futures Water Institute is “dedicated to forecasting and tackling water challenges at all scales, from local communities to national systems.” In the desert Southwest, individual choices add up — here’s where to start.

3% less
water used in Arizona than in 1957 — with 555% more people (ASU Kyl Center)
~20%
irrigation cut in an ASU–Phoenix landscape trial
12%
of home water lost to leaks (EPA)
  • Know your water: find where your water comes from and what’s in it via the EPA’s Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) for your provider.
  • Rethink your yard: replace thirsty grass with desert-adapted (xeriscape) landscaping — ASU + City of Phoenix low-water designs and soil treatments have cut some park irrigation by ~20%.
  • Cut indoor use: shorter showers, turn off the tap while brushing, run full loads, and install EPA WaterSense fixtures — fixing leaks alone saves about 12% of home water.
  • Push for bigger change: ask college or city administrators about irrigation timing, low-water plants, and recycled water — institutional changes scale fastest.
  • Learn & advocate: follow the Kyl Center for Water Policy’s Arizona Water Blueprint — Arizona uses 3% less water today than in 1957, despite 555% more people.
Sources: ASU Global Futures Water Institute & the Kyl Center for Water Policy (globalfutures.asu.edu/water-institute; morrisoninstitute.asu.edu/kyl-center-water-policy; ASU News). Household actions: U.S. EPA WaterSense (epa.gov/watersense).
